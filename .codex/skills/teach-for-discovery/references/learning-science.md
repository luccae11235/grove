# Learning Science Guardrails

Use these principles as design heuristics, not universal laws. Account for prior knowledge, the domain, the time horizon, and the intended transfer. Prefer direct evidence from learner work over assumptions about engagement.

## 1. Guided discovery

Create a bounded search space, a meaningful problem, prompts at decision points, feedback, and a later explicit explanation. Do not confuse learner agency with absence of guidance.

Use a cycle of **attempt → feedback → explanation → new attempt**. Let learners generate an idea when they possess enough pieces to make progress; provide a worked step or hint when search would consume attention without teaching the target relation.

Evidence: a meta-analysis of 164 studies found unassisted discovery generally worse than explicit instruction, while enhanced discovery with scaffolding, feedback, worked examples, or elicited explanations performed better than comparison instruction.

## 2. Cognitive load and multimedia

Protect limited working attention:

- Remove details unrelated to the learning goal.
- Signal the current object, step, and causal relation.
- Place corresponding words and graphics together in space and time.
- Segment a complex process into learner-paced causal units.
- Pre-teach only the few names or components required to follow the mechanism.
- Avoid narrating dense on-screen prose verbatim.
- Keep persistent context visible when later reasoning depends on it.

Do not make material artificially hard. Put effort into retrieval, comparison, explanation, and transfer—not into confusing wording, ornamental animation, or searching the screen.

## 3. Examples, explanations, and fading

Give novices a worked example that exposes decisions and reasons, not only steps. Prompt self-explanation with questions such as “Why is this candidate rejected?” or “What stayed invariant?”

Progress from:

1. fully worked example;
2. completion example with one meaningful blank;
3. similar problem with cues;
4. independent near-transfer problem;
5. contrasting or farther-transfer problem.

Fade guidance as knowledge grows. Extra explanation can become redundant for experienced learners; allow optional depth and faster paths.

## 4. Retrieval, generation, and active learning

Use low-stakes retrieval during and after instruction. Ask learners to produce an answer, explanation, sketch, prediction, or next step before showing it. Retrieval strengthens later access; generating material can outperform merely reading it.

Separate **performance now** from **learning later**. A smooth walkthrough can create familiarity without durable understanding. Revisit core ideas after a delay and in changed contexts when the product supports a sequence of lessons.

Keep active tasks tightly coupled to the intended cognition. Clicking is not active learning unless the learner compares, decides, explains, or solves.

## 5. Analogy and transfer

Map relations rather than appearances. State the source, target, shared relations, prediction, and boundary. Then compare at least two target examples so the learner can abstract the common structure.

Use progressive alignment: begin with examples that share obvious surface and structural similarities, then move toward cases with different surfaces but the same underlying relation.

Never assume that understanding one example transfers automatically. Ask the learner to identify what remains invariant and what changes.

## 6. Motivation and metacognition

Create motivation by making the unresolved question visible. Show why the current tools are insufficient and why the new idea earns its complexity.

Support competence with achievable predictions and informative feedback. Give meaningful control over pace or hypotheses, not a dashboard of arbitrary controls.

Correct fluency illusions. Ask learners to predict their answer, explain without looking, and compare confidence with performance. Do not use difficulty alone as evidence of rigor or ease alone as evidence of clarity.

## 7. Evidence base

- 3Blue1Brown, [advice for math exposition](https://www.3blue1brown.com/about/): begin with concrete examples and the key exercise before general frameworks.
- 3Blue1Brown, [What does it feel like to invent math?](https://www.3blue1brown.com/lessons/inventing-math/): an exemplar of discovery-oriented mathematical exposition.
- Alfieri et al. (2011), [Does Discovery-Based Instruction Enhance Learning?](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021017): meta-analysis distinguishing unassisted from enhanced discovery.
- Mayer (2017), [Using multimedia for e-learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12197): review of coherence, signaling, contiguity, segmenting, pre-training, and related principles.
- Chi et al. (1989), [Self-Explanations](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1302_1): foundational study of how learners explain worked examples to themselves.
- Renkl et al. (1998), [Learning from Worked-Out Examples](https://doi.org/10.1006/ceps.1997.0959): example variability and elicited self-explanation for transferable knowledge.
- Atkinson, Renkl, and Merrill (2003), [Transitioning From Studying Examples to Solving Problems](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.4.774): self-explanation prompts and fading worked steps.
- Roediger and Karpicke (2006), [The Power of Testing Memory](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6916.2006.00012.x): retrieval as a learning event.
- Slamecka and Graf (1978), [The Generation Effect](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.4.6.592): memory benefits from generating rather than only reading.
- Freeman et al. (2014), [Active learning increases student performance in STEM](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1319030111): meta-analysis of active learning in undergraduate STEM.
- Gentner, Loewenstein, and Thompson (2003), [Learning and Transfer](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.2.393): comparison across cases supports schema abstraction and transfer.
- Bjork Learning and Forgetting Lab, [research overview](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/research/): distinction between immediate performance and durable learning, with cautions around desirable difficulties.
