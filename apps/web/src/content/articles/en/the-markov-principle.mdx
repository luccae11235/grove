Freeze a game of Pong while the ball is halfway down the court. Its position is visible, but its direction is not. The same picture could have come from a ball moving upward or a ball moving downward. If the paddle takes the same action in both cases, should we expect the same thing to happen next?

The previous frame resolves the ambiguity because it reveals the ball's direction. That gives us a practical test for a state representation:

> **After the current representation and action are known, can any detail from the history still change our prediction of the next representation or reward?**

A Markov decision process, or MDP, is the standard starting point for formalizing many reinforcement learning problems. It brings together states, actions, rewards, and transition dynamics. At time $t$, the environment's complete state is $s_t$. After the agent chooses $a_t$, the dynamics define a distribution over the next state and reward, $p(s_{t+1},r_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$.

The agent does not always receive that complete state. It may instead receive a chosen representation $z_t$: perhaps a sensor reading, a collection of features, or a summary constructed from the interaction history. Let

$$H_t=(z_0,a_0,r_1,z_1,\ldots,a_{t-1},r_t,z_t)$$

denote everything the agent has observed before choosing $a_t$. The representation $z_t$ is Markovian when adding that history cannot change the predicted distribution:

$$p(z_{t+1},r_{t+1}\mid z_t,a_t,H_t)=p(z_{t+1},r_{t+1}\mid z_t,a_t)$$

The equality is about **history**, not certainty. A transition may remain genuinely random and still be perfectly Markovian. The property fails only when two histories that produce the same $z_t$ and $a_t$ lead to different distributions over what happens next.

<MarkovPropertyComparison />

The Markov property therefore belongs to the process induced by the chosen representation, not to the agent, policy, or learning algorithm. The underlying environment can stay fixed while one representation preserves the property and another aliases situations that require different predictions or decisions.

We will explore that effect in three environments. Each pair keeps the dynamics, rewards, algorithm, hyperparameters, and training budget fixed. Only the representation supplied to the agent changes. The examples will hide three different kinds of information: motion, a remembered event, and progress that gives a transition its reward.

## Pong

Pong gives us the smallest useful version of the opening puzzle. The objective is intuitive, but the reward is sparse: hitting the ball earns a positive reward, missing it earns a negative reward, and every intermediate step earns zero. We will use a small quantized court so that both agents can learn with the same tabular Q-learning algorithm in the browser.

### The MDP

Our first MDP is a simplified Pong environment where a ball crosses a rectangular arena. It bounces off the walls and must be hit by a paddle controlled by the agent at the bottom of the screen. The agent can move the paddle left, stay still, or move right.

We compare two representations. The first contains the ball and paddle positions, $z_t=(x_t,y_t,p_t)$. A ball at one position could be moving up or down, so earlier positions still change the predicted next position. The second representation adds velocity, $z_t=(x_t,y_t,v_t^x,v_t^y,p_t)$. Position and velocity now describe the relevant dynamics of this simplified court; earlier frames add no further predictive information.

<PongConceptFigure kind="environment" />

The first representation violates the Markov property because two histories can reach the same $z_t$ with different hidden velocities. Averaging over those velocities produces a distribution over several possible next positions. That spread is not itself the violation: a stochastic transition can be Markovian. The violation is that the distribution changes when the previous position reveals which velocity is present.

<PongConceptFigure kind="state" />

With position and velocity together, the relevant histories agree about the next-transition distribution. The equality now holds for the simplified dynamics.

The dynamics, reward, launch sequence, Q-learning algorithm, and hyperparameters are identical. Only the observation changes.

This scenario exemplifies what we can call **Missing dynamics**, where the state fails to capture the instantaneous motion required to predict physical transitions.

## The Door and the Key

### The MDP

Our second environment is a small gridworld where the agent must retrieve a key before passing through a door to reach the goal. The key and the door are located on opposite sides of the map, forcing the agent to traverse a central region, collect the key, and then retrace its steps toward the door.

The complete state in this MDP is $s_t=(p_t,k_t)$, where $p_t$ represents the agent's position and $k_t \in \{0, 1\}$ indicates whether the key has been collected. The available actions are $A=\{\text{up}, \text{down}, \text{left}, \text{right}\}$. The door acts as a solid wall when $k_t=0$ and allows passage when $k_t=1$. Reaching the goal yields a positive reward and ends the episode.

<DoorKeyFigure kind="environment" />

We evaluate two representations. In the first, $z_t=p_t$, meaning the agent only knows its current position. In the second, $z_t=(p_t,k_t)$, it knows its position and whether it holds the key.

This presents a different challenge from Pong. There is no missing information regarding instantaneous movement dynamics. If the agent is in an empty cell and moves left, we know exactly which cell it will land in. Instead, the missing information relates to an event that occurred in the past.

Consider the position immediately in front of the door. The same spatial position $p_t$ represents two distinct underlying states: $(p_t,0)$ before the key is collected, and $(p_t,1)$ after the key is collected.

Executing the same action in these two states produces completely different transitions. If $k_t=0$, moving toward the door results in the agent being blocked. If $k_t=1$, the agent steps through. Because the representation $z_t=p_t$ merges these two realities, the transition probability $p(z_{t+1}, r_{t+1} \mid z_t, a_t)$ is forced to represent a mixture of both possible outcomes.

Knowing the history resolves this ambiguity. If the history $H_t$ shows that the agent previously visited the key's location, we know with certainty that $k_t=1$. Consequently, the transition probabilities differ:

$$p(z_{t+1},r_{t+1}\mid z_t,a_t,H_t) \neq p(z_{t+1},r_{t+1}\mid z_t,a_t)$$

This shows the position-only representation violates the Markov property. Adding $k_t$ to the state resolves the issue by compressing a potentially distant historical event into a single current variable. Once $k_t$ is known, the exact path the agent took to get there adds no further relevant information for predicting the next transition.

<DoorKeyFigure kind="state" />

We can refer to this as **Missing memory**. Unlike Pong, the hidden information does not describe physical momentum, but rather summarizes a critical milestone that occurred earlier in the episode.

## Checkpoints

### The MDP

The third environment is a gridworld featuring a central hub connected to three distinct checkpoints: A, B, and C. To successfully complete the episode, the agent must visit these checkpoints in the strict order of $A \rightarrow B \rightarrow C$.

Importantly, no paths are physically blocked or altered during the episode. The geometry of the map and the physical mechanics of movement remain entirely unchanged from start to finish.

The complete state for this MDP is $s_t=(p_t,q_t)$. The variable $p_t$ defines the spatial position, and $q_t \in \{A, B, C, \text{done}\}$ tracks the next expected checkpoint in the sequence. Moving the agent updates its position on the grid. The task state $q_t$ advances only when the correct checkpoint is visited, generating a positive reward.

<MarkovCheckpointFigure kind="environment" />

We evaluate the representations $z_t=p_t$ and $z_t=(p_t,q_t)$.

This scenario presents a subtle counterintuitive challenge. The position $p_t$ alone is perfectly sufficient to predict the physical movement of the agent. For any given position and action, the spatial transition holds true regardless of the past:

$$p(p_{t+1}\mid p_t,a_t,H_t) = p(p_{t+1}\mid p_t,a_t)$$

Knowing the history does not improve our ability to predict where the agent will physically end up next.

However, an MDP defines both the next state and the reward. The current state must be sufficient to determine the reward distribution as well.

Consider entering checkpoint B. If $q_t=B$, entering the cell generates a positive reward and advances the task. If the agent enters that exact same cell while $q_t=A$ or $q_t=C$, the transition yields zero reward. The physical position and the executed action are identical, yet the resulting reward is different.

The variable $q_t$ is entirely dependent on the task history. Knowing which checkpoints have been visited allows us to deduce what reward a specific transition will produce.

While the spatial dynamics are fully Markovian, the reward distribution is not:

$$p(r_{t+1}\mid p_t,a_t,H_t) \neq p(r_{t+1}\mid p_t,a_t)$$

Consequently, the full transition distribution of the MDP violates the Markov property under the reduced representation:

$$p(z_{t+1},r_{t+1}\mid z_t,a_t,H_t) \neq p(z_{t+1},r_{t+1}\mid z_t,a_t)$$

Including $q_t$ makes the current state of the task explicit. Once we know the physical position and the expected checkpoint, the rest of the history is no longer needed to predict the full transition, including the reward.

<MarkovCheckpointFigure kind="reward" />

We categorize this as **Missing reward state**. We are not obscuring physical dynamics, nor are we hiding a condition that enables new physical movements. We are hiding the variable that assigns meaning to a physical transition within the context of the task.

## What was missing?

When we place all three experiments side by side, a clear pattern emerges. In every case, the underlying physical dynamics, the true reward function, the learning algorithm, and the hyperparameters remained completely constant between the two versions. We only altered what the agent considered to be its state.

In the first case, **Missing dynamics**, position failed to inform velocity. The history allowed us to infer the ball's trajectory, thereby altering the predicted distribution of the next spatial state.

In the second case, **Missing memory**, position failed to indicate whether a crucial event had occurred. The history revealed if the key had been collected, dictating which physical transitions were possible moving forward.

In the third case, **Missing reward state**, position perfectly predicted the next movement but failed to determine the reward. The history revealed the agent's progress within the task sequence.

These labels describe what the omitted information changes; they are not mutually exclusive formal categories. Both key status and checkpoint progress, for example, summarize earlier events. The useful question is whether the hidden fact changes physical dynamics, available transitions, rewards, or some combination of them.

<MarkovSynthesisFigure />

These three examples are manifestations of the same mathematical condition. If any detail from the history modifies the predicted distribution of the next representation or reward, even after we know the current representation and action, then the representation is not sufficient:

$$p(z_{t+1},r_{t+1}\mid z_t,a_t,H_t) \neq p(z_{t+1},r_{t+1}\mid z_t,a_t)$$

The entire history would always preserve its own information, but carrying it verbatim is rarely useful. State design aims for a compact sufficient summary: keep the facts from the past that can still change the future, and discard the rest.

In these fixed-seed demonstrations, repairing the aliases allowed the same tabular Q-learning algorithm to produce a more successful greedy policy. That does not mean adding variables always makes learning faster. Irrelevant variables enlarge a tabular state space and can make learning less efficient. The goal is sufficiency, not maximum detail.
